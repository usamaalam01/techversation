## Introduction to Composition Collapse

The concept of composition collapse refers to the phenomenon where AI models with similar performance on individual tasks or questions can exhibit significantly different behaviors when faced with compositional reasoning tasks. This is a critical issue in the development of AI models, as it highlights the limitations of relying solely on aggregate benchmark scores to evaluate their capabilities. In this tutorial, we will explore the implications of composition collapse and provide practical guidance on how to evaluate compositional reasoning in AI models.

## Understanding Compositional Reasoning

Compositional reasoning refers to the ability of an AI model to combine individual pieces of knowledge to arrive at a more complex understanding or solution. This is a crucial aspect of human intelligence, and AI models that can demonstrate compositional reasoning are considered more advanced and versatile. However, as the research paper [arXiv:2605.26789v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26789) highlights, the assumption that a model with better performance on individual tasks will necessarily excel at compositional reasoning is misleading.

## Evaluating Compositional Reasoning

To evaluate compositional reasoning in AI models, we need to move beyond aggregate benchmark scores and focus on more nuanced metrics. One approach is to use multi-hop reasoning tasks, which require the model to combine multiple pieces of knowledge to arrive at a solution. We can also use techniques such as graph-based reasoning, where the model is presented with a graph of entities and relationships and must reason about the connections between them.

### Example Code: Multi-Hop Reasoning

Here is an example of how to implement a multi-hop reasoning task using the Hugging Face Transformers library:
```python
import torch
from transformers import AutoModelForQuestionAnswering, AutoTokenizer

# Load pre-trained model and tokenizer
model = AutoModelForQuestionAnswering.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased")
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased")

# Define a sample question and context
question = "What is the capital of France?"
context = "France is a country in Europe. The capital of France is Paris."

# Tokenize the question and context
inputs = tokenizer(question, context, return_tensors="pt")

# Get the model's answer
answer = model(**inputs)

# Print the answer
print(answer)
```
This code snippet demonstrates how to use a pre-trained BERT model to answer a question that requires multi-hop reasoning. The model must combine the information from the context to arrive at the correct answer.
