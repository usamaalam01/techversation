## Introduction to 'Impossible' Black Holes

The universe is home to a wide range of black holes, from small, stellar-mass black holes formed from the collapse of individual stars, to supermassive black holes found at the centers of galaxies, with masses millions or even billions of times that of the sun. However, there exists a class of black holes that has puzzled scientists for years - mid-sized black holes, often referred to as 'impossible' black holes. These black holes have masses that are too large to be formed from the collapse of a single star, yet too small to be classified as supermassive. Recently, scientists have made a breakthrough in understanding the origin of these enigmatic objects, as reported in a [recent article by Wired](https://www.wired.com/story/the-universe-is-full-of-impossible-black-holes-now-scientists-know-why/).

## The Mystery of Mid-Sized Black Holes

Mid-sized black holes, with masses ranging from approximately 100 to 100,000 solar masses, have been observed in various parts of the universe. However, their formation mechanism has been a topic of debate among astrophysicists. The traditional view is that black holes are formed from the collapse of massive stars, a process that typically results in stellar-mass black holes. On the other hand, supermassive black holes are thought to form through the merger of galaxies, which can bring together multiple stellar-mass black holes, allowing them to grow into supermassive ones. The existence of mid-sized black holes challenges this understanding, as they do not fit neatly into either category.

## Breakthrough in Understanding 'Impossible' Black Holes

The recent discovery, as outlined in the Wired article, provides evidence that these mid-sized black holes can form through the merger of stellar-mass black holes in dense star clusters. This process can occur in the early universe, where star clusters are more common and dense, allowing for frequent mergers of stellar-mass black holes. Over time, these mergers can give rise to mid-sized black holes, which can then grow into supermassive black holes through continued mergers and accretion of surrounding material.
